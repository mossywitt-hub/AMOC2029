INTRO-BELOW-5: Crisis gets more coverage than strategy. So the debate will become louder and more contentious at the same time as the stakes rise and tradeoffs become more difficult. DEPARTMENT |dateline|: November 2029

DEPARTMENT-2: “Defense and finance both want you,” he said. “Urgently. There’s also a scientist in the lobby who says if you don’t see him now he will be forced to leak official information to the press.”
DEPARTMENT-3: “Right.” Miriam said. “I suppose you should send him in.”

DEPARTMENT-5: Miriam was relieved to be getting to work after campaigning through another brutally hot summer. It was hard to project renewal and momentum seven hours into door knocking in 40 degree heat.
DEPARTMENT-6: She was also daunted by the days and weeks ahead. Miriam couldn’t recall the exact point in the campaign when her Department was invented. When it happened it seemed inevitable: the only viable response to ten thousand conversations with voters who had lost a relative in the heat, a livelihood in the drought, property in the flooding that came before.
DEPARTMENT-7: No one in the party could agree on a set of commitments that seemed proportionate to such a broad, uncertain problem. And no one wanted to defend large scale resilience spending to a hostile press. So the Department was born: a catch all for every question, a signal of seriousness and a promise of action that committed them to nothing specific at all.
DEPARTMENT-8: In theory then Miriam had money, officials, the Prime Minister’s personal backing. In reality, she urgently needed a plan.
DEPARTMENT-9 |divider|: divider
DEPARTMENT-10: The scientist was younger than she expected, with a lanyard around his neck and an outfit that gave a hint of school uniform. He pushed a document across her desk and started to speak.
DEPARTMENT-11: “The probability of complete AMOC collapse within fifty years,” he said, “is by some estimates now greater than 50%. That is the finding of our unpublished assessment, based on data from our own systems and from international partners. We think it is possible this collapse happens several times faster, or two times slower. But anyway we don’t need a full collapse. A marked slowdown is enough to foul food planning, flood planning, everything. The consequences would be devastating here, and even worse in most of the places that grow our food. In short, this is not a research paper. It’s a manifesto.”
DEPARTMENT-12: Miriam raised her eyebrows and peered down. There were Atlantic circulation charts and insurance exposure maps; infrastructure modeling and diagrams of cold anomalies pushing across Europe. One graph tracked electricity demand spiking during severe winters. Another showed fishing fleets losing species they had followed for decades. Then the report spelled out the consequences in stark technical language: food shocks under unstable rainfall and import stress; insurance companies refusing to cover climate-vulnerable regions; public systems breaking down like dominoes, one after the other.
DEPARTMENT-13: There was the acronym she realized would contain the Department’s future: AMOC.
DEPARTMENT-14: Miriam knew the basics, of course. A system of currents in the North Atlantic – warm water going north, colder denser water moving south at depth. One of the hidden pieces of machinery that made the land liveable in large swatches of the Northern Hemisphere.
DEPARTMENT-15: The scientist was tapping the page before she finished reading.
DEPARTMENT-16: “My suggestion,” he said, “is that your department starts off by acting as if the reasonable worst-case outcomes are now a lot worse and more likely than anyone has accounted for. You do not have decades left to prepare for an age none of our systems were built for. You have years. If you’re lucky.”

DEPARTMENT-18: Tom was reading over her shoulder. “Finance will ask if any of this is bankable.”
DEPARTMENT-19: “It is,” the scientist said. “But you need to stop planning around the old baselines. The old baselines assume a predictable climate, and that is now a dangerous assumption.”
DEPARTMENT-20: In the early 2020s, policymakers had learned to ask the wrong questions of climate projections. They wanted to know what was most likely, not what plausible worst case they needed to consider in planning. With the AMOC paper in front of her, Miriam realized that the worst case was now much, much worse.

DEPARTMENT-24: She called for a meeting. A discussion of the memo had been scheduled for the following month, but it seemed like time might be in limited supply. She was met with a brief silence when she finished. “So,” the prime minister said at last, steepling tired fingers together. “If we take this seriously what does that actually look like, in policy terms?”
DEPARTMENT-25: The finance minister grimaced but said nothing.
DEPARTMENT-26: “And how about the messaging?” said the energy minister. “Governments have spent half a century telling people that climate change means heatwaves and floods. One in six people still aren’t convinced.[eurobarometer] How can we go out now and say it also means Siberian winters?”
DEPARTMENT-27: Miriam looked through the glass into the government district, where the sky had the raw look of a day that could turn either way.
DEPARTMENT-28: She sighed. “I know. I know that my Department is already the butt of constant jokes from the Right. And I saw the meme about ‘late capitalist diversion tactics’. But all I can tell you is that if we don’t make some hard decisions now, the weather will make them for us.”

Competence won us this mandate, and I can promise you, it will not survive even a few weeks of empty shelves.”
DEPARTMENT-30: When the meeting broke up, Miriam checked her correspondence. Tom had flagged a note from a rural representative complaining that the new government sounded like it was dragging the country into wartime. The foreign ministry wanted language for a call with Beijing, and home affairs had asked for modeling on immigration. She’d made her point.

JOBS-39 |tech|: resilience and jobs
JOBS-39.1: The International Energy Agency’s <a href="https://www.iea.org/reports/world-energy-investment-2025/executive-summary" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">2025 investment update</a> describes a transition that is accelerating unevenly rather than cleanly replacing one system with another. Electricity-sector investment reached roughly $1.5 trillion, some 50% above the $1.1 trillion still flowing into bringing oil, gas and coal to market. Grid investment, at roughly <a href="https://lautec.com/2025/08/28/clean-energy-investment-2025/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">$400 billion annually</a>, remained far below the level required to carry rapid electrification — around a third of what is spent on new generation — held back by permitting delays, transformer and cable supply bottlenecks, and the balance sheets of utilities.
JOBS-39.2: That mismatch creates volatile conditions. Parts of the fossil economy begin declining before replacement infrastructure is capable of carrying the load: a system experiencing contraction, dependency and infrastructure lag at once. This is why reserve capacity arguments are difficult to dismiss on climate grounds alone. During the lag, the plants being subsidized are doing something real.
JOBS-39.3: The jobs question follows from the same lag, but bites through geography. Most projections of the transition show net employment gains. The difficulty is that the gains and the losses do not necessarily land in the same places: fossil employment concentrates in a small number of localities where the industry is also the tax base, the apprenticeship route and the largest employer of people without degrees. Studies of the US workforce, for example, find that <a href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-41133-9" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">location, rather than skills, is the binding constraint</a>: displaced workers frequently <a href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214790X23001855" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">hold transferable competencies</a> but do not live where clean generation is being built. <a href="https://www.iea.org/commentaries/the-importance-of-focusing-on-jobs-and-fairness-in-clean-energy-transitions" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Nor is the exchange like for like.</a> Construction work on a wind farm, for example, may not be a career in the sense a plant job was.
JOBS-39.4: The political consequences are well documented. Regions on the losing side of an industrial transition do not simply decline; they persist in decline and become more likely to elect politicians hostile to the settlement that produced it. This is why transition funding is often fought over regionally rather than nationally.

SUNSHADE |title|: The Sunshade
SUNSHADE |dateline|: April 2031
SUNSHADE-1: If the AMOC memo forced the government to think about energy, flood, and food together, it also forced a question most people still preferred not to think about: what happened when adaptation and emissions cuts looked too slow, and somebody offered to buy time by altering the sky?
SUNSHADE-2: The invitation came on thick creamy paper, the wording precise and tasteful. A private roundtable on climate intervention research and governance. The sponsors were the defense investment organization, a large venture capital fund, and of course the Aurent Foundation—the main philanthropic vehicle for billionaire Philip Aurent.
SUNSHADE-3 |graphic|: invite
SUNSHADE-4: Miriam knew that over the last decade, investor dollars had started to move into solar radiation management and other climate intervention projects—first a trickle, then a steady stream. The Aurent Foundation had quietly match-funded public research projects here and abroad, buying Philip Aurent seats at several important tables without any irritating public scrutiny.
SUNSHADE-5: Tom wanted her to decline immediately.
SUNSHADE-6: “Nothing good has ever come from the phrase ‘private roundtable’,” he said.
SUNSHADE-7: Tom was overcautious of her image and the prime minister wanted eyes in the room, so Miriam left Tom frowning and went.
SUNSHADE-8: It took place in a paneled hall that still smelled faintly of polish. The tables carried name cards for scientists, diplomats, insurance executives, one retired admiral, two philanthropic advisers and Philip Aurent himself, who wore a black suit without ornament, precise on his wiry frame. Two foreign officials joined by secure link, one from Washington and one from Beijing, both insisting they were interested only in the governance side of things.
SUNSHADE-9: Miriam had read the profiles. Aurent had made his money in software infrastructure, then in satellite imaging, then by being early enough in the AI boom to become one of those men governments called about it. He had funded epidemiology at first and, more recently, solar radiation modification research. He flew under the radar for the most part; he didn’t own a private jet.
SUNSHADE-10: “The problem with democratic time,” he was saying now, glowing under the spotlight, “is that it is episodic. You are all smart people; you all know civilization-level risks don’t follow election cycles.”
SUNSHADE-11: A climate modeller from Zurich gave a talk next about stratospheric aerosol injection in deliberately boring language, pointing out that the planet was losing its current sunshade as fuel mixes changed.[hidden-sunshade] Sulphate particles dispersed high in the stratosphere… she made it sound mundane, like frosting on a skylight. Then a lawyer spoke confidently about governance vacuums, and after a tea break an academic told them at length about how the phrase “last resort” had already become a permission structure.
SUNSHADE-12 |tech|: solar radiation modification
SUNSHADE-12.1: Solar radiation modification refers to a family of proposed interventions intended to reflect a small fraction of incoming sunlight back into space in order to reduce warming effects from greenhouse gases. It is not a single technology and remains highly uncertain scientifically, politically and ethically.
SUNSHADE-12.2: Recent research briefings identify two approaches dominating current discussion. The first is Stratospheric Aerosol Injection, which would disperse aerosols or precursor gases into the upper atmosphere to increase planetary reflectivity. The second is Marine Cloud Brightening, which aims to increase the reflectivity of low marine clouds through sea-salt particle spraying.
SUNSHADE-12.3: A deliberate multi-decade intervention program would involve continuous management, uncertain regional side effects and major geopolitical consequences. Two private startups, Make Sunsets and Stardust, have begun testing or developing reflective-particle technologies without dedicated regulatory frameworks. Make Sunsets has already released sulfur dioxide balloons without regulatory approval, selling “cooling credits” to customers; Stardust has raised $75 million to develop a new proprietary particle it claims will be safer than sulfur.  The rapid emergence of private actors is forcing governments to confront whether they will ban such activities outright or create controlled research pathways before commercial firms scale their experiments unilaterally.
SUNSHADE-12.4: This becomes especially relevant under worsening North Atlantic instability. AMOC weakening could alter regional weather patterns, storm tracks and rainfall distributions in ways that are already difficult to model confidently. A large-scale SRM intervention layered on top of those uncertainties could interact unevenly with circulation systems such as the North Atlantic Oscillation and AMOC itself. By the 2034-2036 period, technology, including AI, could be widely used for crisis management but still not governed well enough to function as a full systemic-risk copilot.
SUNSHADE-13: At their table Aurent turned casually to Miriam.
SUNSHADE-14: “Minister,” he said, “at what point does precaution become negligence?”
SUNSHADE-15: Miriam set down her water glass. “Oh, Philip. You’re assuming those are the only categories.”
SUNSHADE-16: Aurent inclined his head. “I’m assuming that if states cannot coordinate under acute risk, someone else eventually tries.”
SUNSHADE-17: “What exactly are you telling me?”
SUNSHADE-18: “We can do research at operational scale,” Aurent said. Miriam noted the polite royal we. “We can have aircraft platforms capable of high-altitude dispersal testing. We can use monitoring systems sophisticated enough to observe atmospheric response in real time. And we can build governance structures that governments could use if they preferred not to begin from zero during an emergency.”
SUNSHADE-19: “And deployment?”
SUNSHADE-20: Around the table, people made minute physical adjustments, pretending not to be listening harder.
SUNSHADE-21: Aurent did not smile. He said, “Not now.”
SUNSHADE-22: The most unnerving thing was the professionalism. Miriam had expected zealotry or a messiah complex. What she was actually looking at was something legible to her: a well thought-out proposal with annexes.
SUNSHADE-23: At the next table, a defense consultant made a note on a napkin and slid it into his jacket pocket. Miriam watched him, feeling her unease grow. How soon would it take for countries to start playing wargames with the global thermostat? Because if an intervention could shift rainfall in one region, it could change it somewhere else. Deliberately or otherwise, somebody else’s drought could become part of your climate policy.
SUNSHADE-24: None of the talks that day mentioned this.
SUNSHADE-25: At lunch she stood in the cloister with a paper cup of coffee. Aurent joined her again without asking.
SUNSHADE-26: “Funny how we keep meeting,” Miriam said, stirring.
SUNSHADE-27: “I’m not trying to corner you,” Aurent said.
SUNSHADE-28: “Aren’t you?”
SUNSHADE-29: “No. I’m trying to corner reality.”
SUNSHADE-30: It was an inane, grandiose sentence. Fit for a billionaire. “That’s the kind of sentence people say before they buy the sky.”
SUNSHADE-31: “It’s already being bought,” Aurent said, “just not in an accountable way. Every delay decision and underfunded adaptation budget is a decision. You only call my side unilateral because it has a face.”
SUNSHADE-32: Miriam could still see that face, glowing under the spotlight as the crowd exhaled adoration, when the scandal broke three days later.
SUNSHADE-33: SECRET SUN-DIMMING TALKS, said the morning papers. One of Miriam’s own party called for her resignation before breakfast. The opposition asked why on earth the government was planning to meddle with the weather rather than cut bills.
SUNSHADE-34 |graphic|: cartoon
SUNSHADE-35: In the legislature, she gave the line the foreign ministry and the prime minister’s office had agreed on: research governance did not imply deployment; any international discussion of climate intervention would have to be transparent, multilateral, and subordinate to emissions reduction and adaptation.
SUNSHADE-36: It had sounded like a good line earlier. Miriam’s voice sounded tinny in the chamber.
SUNSHADE-37: That evening Tom read out the damage from the private polling without saying I told you so: “Your support is dropping with everyone except people who already think the entire century is a systems failure.”
SUNSHADE-38: “Comforting.”
SUNSHADE-39: “In fairness,” he said kindly, “that is a growing demographic.”
SUNSHADE-40: The trouble did not go away. Scientists who wanted cautious public oversight blamed her for legitimizing the subject and climate campaigners accused the government of searching for a technological excuse. Two think tanks produced serious reports within the week, one arguing that responsible states had a duty to investigate all options, the other arguing that investigation at this stage was already a form of normalization. The prime minister told Miriam privately that some issues arrived before the headlines wanted to understand them, and those were usually the ones that ate political parties alive.
SUNSHADE-41: One night, driving back from a regional visit, Miriam looked up through the car window and saw an aircraft high over cloud, its vapor trail clean and thin against the evening.
SUNSHADE-42: It made her unexpectedly sad.
SUNSHADE-43: That someone somewhere now looked up at the same sky, and saw a system that might soon need adjusting.

SCENE IV  —  THE SEAM BETWEEN THE SYSTEMS   ·   key: seam
================================================================
SEAM |title|: The Seam Between the Systems
SEAM |dateline|: August 2032
SEAM-1: The first major flood did not settle any scientific argument. It did something simpler: it drowned the ground floor of a hospital, knocked out power to a pumping station, and trapped two hundred families in homes they could no longer insure or refinance; it did all this before lunch.[flood-hospitals]
SEAM-2: For two days the weather had been described on television as erratic, which secretly meant very bad and yet too compound to fit inside a single warning color.
SEAM-3: Miriam was in the national crisis room before dawn, hair unwashed, wearing the same purple sweater she kept in her office for votes that ran late. The screens showed different kinds of water monitoring: Atlantic pressure systems rotating across satellite loops, river gauges flashing amber and red, storm-surge modeling crawling up maps in luminous colors. The room looked nearly festive.
SEAM-4: On one monitor, a presenter in a yellow coat stood on a sea wall and shouted calmly into a microphone while the wind tried to fold her in half.
SEAM-5: They did not have long to wait. The first failures always came in the seams between systems. A coastal pumping station lost power during peak inflow. Surface-water drains already saturated by days of rain began forcing water back uphill through older networks. Roads flooded first, followed by basement apartments, then sewage systems.
SEAM-6: And then by dawn the roads behind the seafront seemed to be rearranged into canals, the salt water moving through storefronts carrying trash cans and fragments of fencing. Cars sat half-submerged at impossible angles, balancing themselves in the new daylight.
SEAM-7: At eleven the barrier closed and still the river kept arriving anyway, swallowing everything up in its relentless steady wake.
SEAM-8: People carried valuables in laundry baskets. Someone pushed a freezer chest up a hill with three neighbors braced behind it. Miriam watched one clip of a little girl in a school sweatshirt, holding a rabbit carrier safely above her head.
SEAM-9: The room used the proper bureaucratic terms: multi-source flooding, infrastructure coordination pressure, and studiously avoided the term climate event. Miriam’s finance-trained mind usually respected precision. But the language seemed to flatten everything into process. This was water entering electrical systems. Water entering homes and climbing up the stairs.
SEAM-10: At half past eleven the prime minister asked, “How bad if the next tide arrives before the water is out?”
SEAM-11: No one answered.
SEAM-12: An hour later they received confirmation of two flood-related deaths in a basement apartment above the old harbor road.
SEAM-13: Miriam went to the coast the next morning, driving through villages where grit and reeds had been left to rot in hedges. The air had that unmistakable post-flood smell: fuel, bleach, river mud.
SEAM-14: The church became an evacuation center because it was on slightly higher ground. Miriam opened the doors to loud chatter and foil blankets, charging strips, six trays of grocery store sandwiches. A local official with her hair pinned back tightly handed Miriam a visitor vest and told her not to stand in the way of distribution.
SEAM-15: She met a man who had spent the night lifting his elderly mother from one chair to another. She shook hands with a boy in gym shorts carrying bottled water to older residents. A volunteer showed Miriam a side room where people were sleeping between interviews with housing officers. SEAM-20 |tech|: why a flood leads to insurance, lending, and politics
SEAM-20.1: The issue is not that AMOC causes a single flood event directly, but that it changes the climatic context in which tides, rainfall, surge and infrastructure vulnerability interact.
SEAM-20.2: By the early 2030s, major compound floods could be triggered by overlapping Atlantic storm activity, high tides, elevated mean sea level and intense rainfall—all conditions to which an AMOC slowdown could contribute. SEAM-20.3: The insurance consequences follow rapidly. By 2032-2033, insurance repricing could accelerate after major losses; some postal codes could become effectively uninsurable. BORDER-25 |tech|: climate displacement and the border
BORDER-25.1: Climate-related migration has been recognized and modeled for decades. In 2007, for example, the <a href="https://www.lse.ac.uk/granthaminstitute/publication/the-economics-of-climate-change-the-stern-review/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Stern Review</a> identified “large-scale population movement” as a critical climate-related risk for governments to consider. Estimates of potential numbers are contentious, especially as severe climate impacts continue to manifest faster than many models predicted.
BORDER-25.2: Recent assessments such as <a href="https://www.europarl.europa.eu/RegData/etudes/BRIE/2021/698753/EPRS_BRI(2021)698753_EN.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">this briefing</a> published by the European Parliamentary Research Service, highlight dramatic recent shifts in internal migrations. In 2024, the Internal Displacement Monitoring Center (IDMC) recorded 65.8 million new displacements of which 45.8 million were the result of floods, windstorms, earthquakes or droughts—the highest annual figure ever recorded by the IDMC.
BORDER-25.3: Accordingly, the World Bank’s <a href="https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2021/09/13/climate-change-could-force-216-million-people-to-migrate-within-their-own-countries-by-2050" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Groundswell modeling</a> projects up to 216 million people could be forced to migrate within their own countries by 2050 across six world regions, with hotspots emerging as early as 2030, driven by water scarcity, crop failure and sea-level rise.
BORDER-25.4: It is therefore reasonable to assume that the very severe effects of a slowing AMOC would lead to mass displacements both intraregionally and globally.
BORDER-25.5: A domestic population already absorbing climate losses (e.g. flood payouts, mortgage stress, insurance repricing) might very well treat new arrivals as competitors for the same finite budget. While the UNHCR’s interpretation of Article 31 of the Refugee Convention opposes penalizing qualifying refugees merely for irregular entry, governments could make access much harder, rely more heavily on safe-third-country arrangements, and restrict the statuses granted.

GRID-TECH |tech|: why rooftop panels, batteries and chargers became a political bloc
GRID-TECH.1: Distributed energy resources are no longer a niche interest. GROUND |dateline|: March 2034

GROUND-21 |tech|: the tactics of magnifying uncertainty
GROUND-21.1: Magnifying points of uncertainty or disagreement between scientists is a tactic that has been deployed repeatedly by industries such as tobacco, fossil energy, and chemical manufacturing: internal documents from tobacco firms famously stated that “doubt is our product”. Writers such as Naomi Oreskes and Erik Conway have documented how the same small network of scientists, PR agencies and lobby groups applied the “manufactured doubt” strategy to climate science: questioning methods, cherry-picking minority views, demanding impossible proof and attacking researchers personally. The goal is to confuse the debate sufficiently that overwhelming consensus cannot be achieved. Instead, the public feels uncertain and opinions divide along familiar party lines. This has proved highly effective in delaying regulation, sometimes by decades.
GROUND-22: Philip Aurent, who had not deployed anything and had not gone away, sent Miriam another note after one especially bad agriculture forecast.
GROUND-23 |italic|: You cannot adaptation-budget your way out of a circulation problem.
GROUND-24: Miriam did not respond.
GROUND-25: By the weeks before the election, after endless debates with voters and grillings in the press, she had the shape of her own answer, if not yet the mandate to enact it. You plan anyway. You drag grain reserves, insurance frameworks, crop science, and borrowing costs into the same room whether departments like it or not. You stop asking the land to pay for every transition through private leverage. You make the country slightly more expensive in the present so that the future remains possible.
GROUND-26 |tech|: the agricultural plan
GROUND-26.1: Research from multiple sources shows an increasing risk of simultaneous breadbasket failures for major crops, with evidence that the risk of low production across major producing regions has risen due to climatic extremes.
GROUND-26.2: By 2028-2029, multi-region crop failures could cut yields by at least 10% across three or more breadbaskets in the same season, triggering export bans, hoarding, and repeated food-price spikes in import-dependent economies. By 2030-2031, global water bankruptcy could become an explicit organizing concept in international reporting, with aquifer depletion, glacier retreat, and flood-drought whiplash interacting across major basins.
GROUND-26.3: The World Bank's March 2026 food security update tied food insecurity to the interaction of conflict and climate shocks. The United Nations University's 2026 Global Water Bankruptcy report argued that around 70% of major aquifers were in long-term decline, with more than 170 million hectares of irrigated cropland under high or very high water stress, and more than half of global food production concentrated in areas where total water storage was already declining or unstable.
GROUND-26.4: The UK's national security assessment, published by the joint intelligence committee in early 2026, warned that “every critical ecosystem is on a pathway to collapse” and that key earth systems may cross irreversible tipping points within the next two decades. The assessment identified the Amazon and Congo Basin rainforests, North American and Eurasian boreal forests, the Himalayan biome, and the mangroves and coral reefs of Southeast Asia and the South Pacific as systems whose collapse could have “catastrophic” consequences for human food, water and natural resource systems, and concluded that without significant increases in resilience, it is unlikely the UK would be able to maintain food security if ecosystem collapse drives geopolitical competition for food.
GROUND-26.5: A marked AMOC slowdown would compound many of these factors. In Europe, a slowdown could shift North Atlantic storm tracks, driving both intense summer droughts and extreme rainfall, disrupting sowing and harvesting windows. This would suppress the yields of staple terrestrial crops and degrade pastures for livestock. Harsh rainfall reductions <a href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s43016-019-0011-3" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">could even make agricultural irrigation economically infeasible</a>.
GROUND-26.6: In the ocean, a slowdown could trigger a decline in marine primary production that heavily impacts fish development and commercial fisheries in the North Atlantic and Barents Sea.
GROUND-26.7: Together, these compounding shocks to both crop viability and fishery yields would force Europe to increasingly rely on global food imports, just as the same slow down could be disrupting production in North American and Asian hubs, and across the tropical belt, rapidly driving up global food prices.
GROUND-26.8: In this setting, an agricultural plan is not just about yields; it is about whether the hydrology, credit system, and state capacity still line up well enough to produce confidence for the next planting season: the “sovereign triple-squeeze” of debt, adaptation costs and disaster losses.
GROUND-27: Standing with the farmer on the rise above the fields, Miriam thought again of Nina at six crunching with pleasure into her apple, of all the small ordinary futures that depended on getting the math right on these small ordinary fights.

Streets with active community energy programs moved to her, remembering grid connection dates and local bill relief and local organizers who knew exactly which cul-de-sacs to knock. The early looks at the old marina district were ugly.
VOTE-22: At half one the officials asked candidates to remain available.
VOTE-23: At two seventeen Nina sent a message from university: Whatever happens, get some sleep.
VOTE-24: At two forty-three the final verified bundles were carried over.
VOTE-25: At three the result was ready.
VOTE-TECH |tech|: why the election was also a solvency vote
VOTE-TECH.1: By the point this story reaches its election, the argument is not only environmental or moral; it is explicitly about solvency and state capacity. UNDRR’s 2025 Global Assessment Report says disaster costs now exceed $2.3 trillion annually when cascading and ecosystem costs are included, and describes a destructive cycle of disasters, debt, uninsurability and humanitarian need. Its summary is blunt about the public-finance channel: households lose assets and income, tax revenues shrink, governments borrow more, and as debt becomes riskier the room to fund recovery narrows.
VOTE-TECH.2: That framing is increasingly echoed in finance-policy institutions. A 2026 Grantham Research Institute analysis argues that early adaptation investment can bolster fiscal stability, reduce borrowing costs and mitigate debt deterioration, while warning that climate damages are already significant and likely underestimated because many analyses still omit cascading and compounding risks, flooding, and the full impacts of tropical cyclones. The report finds economic benefit–cost ratios of around 4:1 and internal rates of return of about 25% for adaptation investment.
VOTE-TECH.3: That is why insurance, food systems, flood defenses, borrowing costs, and emissions policy collapse into one political argument. Emergency politics emerge when repeated losses impair insurance and housing markets. By 2032-2033, some postal codes could become effectively uninsurable. By 2034-2036, housing, insurance and mortgage markets could show structural impairment in exposed regions, and the sovereign triple-squeeze – debt, adaptation costs, disaster losses – become acute in many vulnerable states. What elections decide in that context is not simply the direction of travel, but whether institutions adapt before compounding losses start governing for them.

ENDING  —  THE GOVERNMENT RETURNS   ·   key: good

ENDING-GOOD |label|: The Government Returns
ENDING-GOOD-1 |lead-italic|: It is just enough, just.
ENDING-GOOD-1 |lead-rest|:  With a majority smaller than anyone likes, the government returns reduced and brittle. Miriam comes back with dark circles under her eyes and a folder waiting from the executive on day one.
ENDING-GOOD-2: The department’s mandate is renewed and expanded. After months of argument it is rebranded as a National Climate Resilience Center – boring, but a decent acronym. It gives the government one place where flood maps, grid stress, insurance withdrawals, migration scenarios, and public-health thresholds are forced to work together before any sort of crisis.
ENDING-GOOD-3: Farming subsidies stay, though amended from what was first proposed. The flood defense program is extended and linked to building regulations that actually force developers to listen. The international talks on climate intervention do not solve the problem of private actors like Philip Aurent, but they force him into a room with states and an inspection regime, enough visibility that rogue action becomes harder, at least for a while.
ENDING-GOOD-4: Nothing feels especially triumphant. There are worse floods. There are colder anomalies in a few winters than people of Miriam’s parents’ generation remember from childhood, and hotter summers than their grandparents would have thought unbearable. Insurance companies still retreat in more and more areas. Some coastal towns are abandoned in increments. The AMOC brief changes title twice and then simply becomes one of those standing papers governments carry responsibly across administrations.
ENDING-GOOD-5: But some things are built in time, and the idea spreads. Not everyone copies the same model – adaptation is always local – but the core of the concept, at least: a permanent institution where long-term risks will not be ignored when it comes time to talk about budgets.
ENDING-GOOD-6: And once a government budgets seriously for survival, it has to keep finding itself budgeting for a future worth surviving for. Cutting emissions, drawing carbon down, and protecting wetlands are all just different ways of lowering the cost of the storm.
ENDING-GOOD-7: By the middle of the century Miriam is dead three years and Nina works in a community health hub where the building has floodable ground-floor storage, cooling, a roof thick with panels and batteries. She is not living in a saved world and in fact she is living in a harder one, but it is one that still contains systems which function often enough to be relied on. The clinic can stay open and cool through hot summers. Patients with asthma and heart failure are called in before pressure spikes and cold snaps because the local weather data system speaks to the grid and they both talk to the local social services team, and, miraculous in its ordinariness, they all still recognize each other as part of the same public.
ENDING-GOOD-8: Some evenings, cycling home along the estuary path, Nina thinks of her mother not as history but as a way of governing she has mostly had to infer from anecdotes and photographs. Her mother in a waterproof coat on a farm. Her mother in borrowed boots beside floodwater.
ENDING-GOOD-9: She has children of her own. She tells them their grandmother “helped us with the weather.” And because the children have grown up in a world where battery status, school heat alerts, flood texts, and food standards are part of the fabric of a day, they understand more than Miriam would once have expected.

ENDING  —  THE GOVERNMENT FALLS   ·   key: bad
================================================================
ENDING-BAD |label|: The Government Falls
ENDING-BAD-1: Miriam loses by about seven hundred votes. Through the long night of the count, she could see every close race slipping away, one by one, and nothing she could do to stop it.
ENDING-BAD-2: The new administration does not deny climate risk; it does something more damaging. The department is folded back into old silos before it can become the permanent center Miriam had argued for. Floods go back to one department, food to another, energy to the market, technology to private initiative. Long-tail risk goes back to the category of things that can be revisited after the cost of living crisis and “still uncertain” returns as a governing phrase.
ENDING-BAD-3: Worker support for fossil fuel regions gets more money in the short term, but there’s no pressure to build new industries fast enough to replace the old ones. The agricultural plan is cut back to grants that farmers have to take out loans to access. The adaptation budgets are raided twice to pay for other things. Border politics harden under worsening conditions, with more people moving away from coastlines and fire corridors. Police set up strict checkpoints on inland roads; some towns deny school enrollment to children from flooded areas.
ENDING-BAD-4: Philip Aurent stays outside government, pouring money into projects that do not make the headlines.
ENDING-BAD-5: Years later, after another run of crop failures and a diplomatic scramble over atmospheric monitoring, a coalition of states and private funders authorizes an intervention package Miriam would once have described as ungovernable. It buys time in some places, perhaps, but rearranges pressure in others. Arguments about causation become scientists’ entire careers. No one trusts the rain and so the old certainty – the sky – goes quietly.
ENDING-BAD-6: By the middle of the century Nina works in an emergency health unit moved inland from the coast after the second retreat. The building is undersized and always full. In the waiting room sit people with heat stress, respiratory complications, severe diarrhea after water interruptions. The buses still run most days; there are schools and supermarkets; but the strain has entered everything; systems fall flat like jokes from a generation ago.
ENDING-BAD-7: Sometimes, at the end of a shift, when another storm briefing lights up every phone in the unit and someone mutters that the warnings are always in force now so what difference, Nina takes out the photograph of Miriam from her locker. It was taken in a field years before, her mother half turned away from the camera talking to someone out of frame. The horizon behind her is bright.
ENDING-BAD-8: She thinks about the years in which people mistook uncertainty for safety. She thinks about how much damage can be done by asking for more research when what you really want is more time, another year, another election in which nobody has to say out loud that the old baselines are gone.
ENDING-BAD-9: When her own daughter asks one evening whether things had really once been easier, she says, “Yes. Easier.” Not simpler, but: “Less expensive. Less– less exhausting.” She can’t quite capture it.
ENDING-BAD-10: “What changed?”
ENDING-BAD-11: Nina considers the maps on the wall, the old ones showing green farmland where now there was salt-tolerant scrub. She thinks of the winter last year when the temperature dropped so fast that pipes burst in half the town. She thinks of the summer before that, when the cooling stations ran out of space. She thinks of the fact that every family now keeps an extensive domestic emergency kit by their front door without thinking itself unusual.
ENDING-BAD-12: “Not one thing,” she says at last.
ENDING-BAD-13: And later, after the child is asleep, she stands at the kitchen window and watches weather move over the dark.

ANNEX I  —  WHY THIS EXISTS
================================================================
WHY-1: We created this resource for three reasons:
WHY-2 |bullets|
WHY-2.1: To help people understand a new era of climate change politics.
WHY-2.2: To raise awareness of underestimated climate risks and tipping points, with AMOC slowdown as a specific example.
WHY-2.3: To highlight the positive outcomes that are achievable and suggest ways for individuals to contribute.
WHY-3: We believe climate politics is entering a new phase around the globe, fueled by changes in the weather right now and a long term risk picture that is worsening fast.
WHY-4: There is a sense that people have stopped caring about climate change. A lot of evidence suggests that this is not the case. In fact, in many countries, more people report being more worried than ever.[climate-concern] Many describe foreboding or even despair about the future. This is telling: it implies that we aren't facing a denial of the problem any more, but instead, a lack of belief that it can be solved.
WHY-5: This might seem strange at a point when a lot of the technology we need to mitigate climate change is widely available, cheap, being deployed out faster than anyone anticipated, and largely popular. Intentional deny-delay tactics overstating clean downsides are a part of the reason. But so are the decades in which the climate has been a headline issue again and again, yet limited progress has been made toward a solution.
WHY-6: The politics of climate change are extremely difficult. There is no precedent for solving a collective action problem (or “tragedy of the horizon”) of such complexity. The inertia of our current energy system is huge—it keeps most of us alive every day. Painstaking multilateral decision making processes like COP decay toward localization: claims that “our country is only y% of global emissions, so it doesn’t matter” on the one side; repositioning renewables in an energy security agenda on the other.
WHY-7: The politics of resilience are different because resilience is inherently local. It is about individual countries, regions, and communities: local farms, reservoirs, sea defenses, food supply, and so on. The trade offs are more complicated, with mitigation and resilience sometimes at odds: should a gas plant be retired, for example, or retained for capacity in extreme heat and cold waves?
WHY-8: Mitigation is intangible and long term; resilience is about the crisis in front of us. This resource looks at how this will be likely to play out, once an extreme risk profile forces a democratic government to react. We have kept the political vocabulary and geography intentionally non specific to avoid pinning the narrative to just one country.
WHY-9: AMOC is central to the story and a very real threat, which we want readers to be informed about. But even if we collectively get lucky in relation to AMOC—if we avoid a severe slowdown or collapse—complex and severe climate risks are already here. It is a matter of time before their impacts in cost, disruption, and human suffering rise to a point that forces governments to respond in more coordinated ways.
WHY-10: Critically, the outcomes that remain achievable are not just not bad, but better than the present. They may not be as dramatic as AI futurism but they are more substantive: cleaner, quieter towns and cities; politics free from energy crises and price shocks; generations that don’t have to live out fundamental anxiety about the future (or at least, not climate-related anxiety).
WHY-11: Everything we depict in this resource is grounded in climate science—we provide technical explainers throughout—and informed by decades of experience at the front line of climate politics.
WHY-12: That experience has taught us to be careful with predictions. This is not one. It is a framework for thinking about a likely reality and the choices we might get to make.

ANNEX II  —  AMOC BRIEFING NOTE
================================================================
AMOC-1: The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) is a system of ocean currents that moves water, and the heat carried in that water, around the Atlantic. Warm, salty water flows north near the surface from the tropics, shedding heat and becoming saltier as it travels. Cooled and now denser, it sinks in the seas near Greenland and the North Atlantic, then returns southward at depth.
AMOC-2: The Gulf Stream is one limb of this system. Together with the broader northward transport of ocean heat and prevailing atmospheric circulation, it helps give northwest Europe much milder winters than other regions at similar latitudes.
AMOC-3 |heading|: Consequences of disruption
AMOC-4: The AMOC is projected to weaken as the climate warms, and there is a growing concern that it could collapse entirely. The consequences could be profound. Northwest Europe could experience substantial cooling, particularly in winter, with much more severe cold extremes. This seemingly paradoxical cooling in a warming world would result from the sharp reduction in northward ocean heat transport.
AMOC-5: Further south, the disruption would be felt as hunger: by shifting the Intertropical Convergence Zone, a weakened AMOC weakens the West African Monsoon and dries the Sahel, while disrupting the monsoon on which much of South Asia depends for agriculture. The same circulation changes intensify drought and fire risk in parts of the Amazon, adding to pressures that could push the rainforest toward its own tipping point of irreversible dieback. In the ocean, nutrient transport falters and marine productivity and food webs are disrupted. AMOC weakening also affects the ocean’s capacity to absorb the CO₂ we continue to emit.[worse-outcome]
AMOC-6 |heading|: The climate link
AMOC-7: The mechanism behind this weakening sits in the wider context of a planet steadily accumulating heat. On average, surface temperatures of 1.5–1.6°C above baseline have already been observed through the 2020s, suggesting that the planet is warming faster than expected.
AMOC-8: That accumulated heat does not distribute evenly, and the AMOC is one of the major systems it disturbs. Melting Greenland ice and increased rainfall add fresh water to the northern region where sinking occurs. Fresher, warmer water is less dense and more reluctant to sink, which is the lever by which warming can slow the circulation.
AMOC-9 |heading|: Tipping points
AMOC-10: This is where the idea of a tipping point becomes relevant. We tend to assume that a little more cause produces a little more effect. Some systems do not work that way. They absorb stress and then flip into a different state, often abruptly and often without an easy way back. The AMOC is regarded as one such system: there may be a threshold beyond which the circulation reorganizes into a much weaker mode. Reversing the forcing that triggered the transition may not be sufficient to restore the previous circulation state.
AMOC-11: Moreover, the effects of a slowing AMOC would be likely to compound. The AMOC interacts with food systems, energy demand, insurance markets, coastal infrastructure and sovereign finance, for example. It is a textbook example of a systemic, compounding risk, in which strain on one system raises the odds of failure in the next.
AMOC-12 |figure|: Map of interactions between climate tipping elements, Wunderling et al 2024.
AMOC-13 |heading|: Probability
AMOC-14: Assessments diverge significantly on timing and likelihood of AMOC weakening and collapse. The IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report judged it very likely that the AMOC will weaken during the twenty-first century under all emissions scenarios. It did not make a confident claim about the timing of any abrupt change.
AMOC-15 |heading|: Risk thinking
AMOC-16: Risk thinkers like insurers have frameworks for understanding low-probability, high-severity risk. The instinct on hearing that an event is “uncertain” or “only a few percent likely this century” is to file it under “probably won't happen”. For catastrophic, largely irreversible outcomes, that instinct is problematic. We do not insure our homes because fire is likely; we insure against the size of the loss. Probability and severity have to be weighed together.[risk-thinking]
AMOC-17 |heading|: Further reading
AMOC-18: There are many excellent resources for further reading on the AMOC, for example <a href="https://interactive.carbonbrief.org/amoc-explainer/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">this explainer</a> from Carbon Brief, <a href="https://arcinit.substack.com/p/the-drivers-of-amocs-destabilization" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">this summary</a> from ARC, and <a href="https://www.noc.ac.uk/discover-the-ocean/oceans-explained/the-atlantic-meridional-overturning-circulation" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">this microsite</a> from the UK’s National Oceanography Center.

COMPLEX SYSTEMS NOTE
================================================================
CS-1: Climate change is a massive system complexity problem twice over. Its cause is the fuels we burn for energy, and energy is fundamental to everything we do. And it affects our physical climate—weather and water and land and the life in it—which, in turn, underpins everything, including our own survival.
CS-2: When Leonard Read pointed out in 1958 that no one knows how to make a pencil, he was celebrating the free market. But his point is usually remembered as one about system complexity. In his essay the pencil tells us in its own words that its journey began with the felling of a tree:
CS-3 |quote|: Now contemplate all the saws and trucks and rope and the countless other gear used in harvesting and carting the cedar logs to the railroad siding. Think of all the persons and the numberless skills that went into their fabrication: the mining of ore, the making of steel and its refinement into saws, axes, motors.
CS-4: Logging and oil extraction are more politically charged as metaphors than they were seventy years ago, but the point stands. No one of us can fathom the complexity behind the simple things we use every day, let alone the systems, natural and synthetic, that sustain our every need.
CS-5: Leonard Read is right to point out the remarkable spontaneous order of complex systems. But those systems have other features too. They are hard to think about, since maintaining even a greatly simplified mental model is impossible. They are hard to measure, and even harder to predict. They have enormous physical inertia: billions of objects made and moving every second. And they have an inertia of imagination. It’s tricky to even think about systems that are greatly different from the ones that envelop us.
CS-6: Counterintuitively, they are also very fragile. Small changes propagate in strange and unpredictable ways with hugely outsized effects.
CS-7: Finally, we are ourselves an inescapable part of these systems. We can’t opt out any more than we can opt out of the climate itself.
CS-8: All of this goes some way to explaining why climate change is such an intractable problem. Hard to measure, hard to predict, hard to even think about. But that same complexity is the key to understanding how we can navigate a safe path forward. We hope that this resource helps readers think about effective decision making under high climate uncertainty, and about how to contribute as individuals to a safe and optimistic future.

RESOURCES  —  WHAT YOU CAN DO   ·   key: what-you-can-do
================================================================
WYCD-1 |heading|: Talk to the people who represent you
WYCD-2: Climate resilience only becomes a priority for politicians when they hear that it matters to the people who elect them. That can mean asking a canvasser what their party will do about flooding on your street, writing to your representative at national or state level, turning up to a local planning hearing, or raising it at a neighborhood meeting. None of these feels dramatic, and the effect of any one of them is small, but elected representatives do count the letters.
WYCD-3 |heading|: Learn to recognize delay, not just denial
WYCD-4: Outright denial has largely faded from mainstream political debate in most wealthy countries. What has replaced it is a set of arguments for postponement: that responsibility lies elsewhere (usually China), that some non-disruptive technology will handle it, that the costs of acting fall unfairly on ordinary people, or that it is now too late to bother. Researchers led by William Lamb catalogued these in a 2020 paper, <a href="https://doi.org/10.1017/sus.2020.13" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Discourses of climate delay</a> — worth twenty minutes, because once you can name the four moves you notice them everywhere, including in arguments you agree with.
WYCD-5 |heading|: Give money, ideally regularly
WYCD-6: There are more resilience causes worth funding than most people realize: ecosystem restoration (peatlands, saltmarsh, mangroves, floodplain reconnection); urban heat resilience, which mostly means shade, trees and cool roofs in the poorest districts of hot cities; clean energy access in low-income countries; policy advocacy and strategic litigation; agricultural resilience and seed diversity; indigenous land stewardship, which has an unusually good record per hectare protected; independent climate science and monitoring; and disaster organizations that work on preparedness rather than only response. It's worth splitting between work you can see and work you can't — restoration is verifiable and satisfying, while advocacy, litigation and research probably carry higher leverage per dollar but will never give you a clear answer about whether it worked. Picking two or three and sticking with them beats spreading thinly.
WYCD-7: A note on how you give. Small restoration trusts and advocacy organizations can only hire people if they can predict next year's income, so a standing order of $10 a month is worth considerably more to them than $120 at Christmas. Most countries offer some form of tax relief on charitable giving, and it is usually worth the ten minutes it takes to claim.
WYCD-8 |heading|: Look at where your pension and savings are invested
WYCD-9: For most people of working age, retirement savings are by a wide margin the largest pot of capital they have any influence over — far larger than their household emissions in any plausible accounting. Find out what your default fund actually holds, switch if your plan offers a better option, and if you want to go further, ask your employer's plan trustees or investment committee what their stewardship and voting policy is.
WYCD-10: A technical note, because this field is murkier than it is usually presented. Most mainstream ESG and divestment-oriented funds work by selling shares on secondary markets, which transfers ownership to a less scrupulous buyer without depriving the company of any capital. The evidence that this raises a polluter's cost of capital enough to change behavior is weak, and the sector has a real greenwashing problem. A small allocation to primary-market impact investing — where your money funds an asset that would not otherwise exist — probably does more per dollar. Platforms such as <a href="https://www.energiseafrica.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Energise Africa</a> (UK) and <a href="https://www.lendahand.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Lendahand</a> (EU) finance solar home systems in sub-Saharan Africa; in the US, <a href="https://calvertimpact.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Calvert Impact</a> offers something structurally similar; community solar and green bond funds exist in most markets. These carry real capital risk, are often illiquid, and are not deposit-protected. None of this is financial advice, and anyone considering it should look at the terms properly or speak to an adviser.
WYCD-11 |heading|: Consider the decisions you make at work
WYCD-12: Most of us spend far more hours as a professional than as a consumer, and our professional decisions are the ones we tend not to think of as climate decisions at all. A planning officer, a doctor, a procurement lead, a school board member, a landlord, a structural engineer: each has a set of choices that shapes how well their part of the world copes with heat and water. If you think you might have influence of this kind and would like to talk it through, please get in touch — we want to hear from people in these roles, and we can often help you work out where the real leverage sits.
WYCD-13 |heading|: Find out what your street is exposed to, and who lives on it
WYCD-14: Most countries publish flood and wildfire hazard maps; find yours and pay particular attention to surface water and stormwater flooding, which catches people out far more often than rivers do. Then do the less obvious half: work out who nearby is elderly, unwell or living alone. Eric Klinenberg's study of the 1995 Chicago heatwave found that neighborhoods with similar poverty and demographics had very different death rates, and that the difference was largely down to whether people knew one another well enough to knock on a door.
WYCD-15 |heading|: Say what you think, because most people assume they are in a minority
WYCD-16: A 2024 study across 125 countries found a large gap between how willing people say they are to act on climate and how willing they believe their fellow citizens are — the average person substantially underestimates everyone else. This means a great many people are staying quiet in the mistaken belief that they are unusual. Mentioning it at work, at the school gate, in the bar or in a group chat is not a soft substitute for real action; it corrects a specific and measurable false belief.
WYCD-17 |heading|: Buy the low-carbon option while it is still slightly too expensive
WYCD-18: A heat pump, an induction hob, a used electric car or a proper retrofit will not make a meaningful dent in national emissions on their own, and we would rather not pretend otherwise. What early buyers actually do is pay down the cost curve: German households buying solar panels in 2005 at absurd prices are the reason a family in Bangladesh can afford them now. Framed accurately, this is less about shrinking your own footprint and more about making something affordable for someone you will never meet.
